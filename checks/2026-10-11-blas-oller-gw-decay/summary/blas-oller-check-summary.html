Do gravitational waves decay into light?

An independent check of D. Blas and J. A. Oller, “Gravitational waves decay in vacuum,” arXiv:2607.24930 (version 2)

Prepared for Ethan Van Winkle · 27 October 2026 · From the Science repository, github.com/EthanDJohnson/Science, folder checks/2026-10-11-blas-oller-gw-decay/
How this was made

The check was run by AI agents (Anthropic’s Claude models), directed from the Science repository. Several independent analyses attacked the paper from different directions, adversarial referees challenged each one, and a final critic re-ran the scripts. Every number below was printed by a script whose output log is kept beside it. No physicist has reviewed it yet. Read it as a careful draft of a referee report, not as a published result. Where it is uncertain, it says so.

The short version

  • The claim. Treat a gravitational wave as a coherent state of N gravitons. Two gravitons can fuse into two photons (g g → γγ). Each fusion is suppressed by G², but the rate grows as N², so the authors find that gravitational waves decay into photon pairs in empty space. They apply it to binary mergers, the stochastic gravitational-wave background and dark matter, with an extra boost from the photons of the cosmic microwave background (CMB).
  • Their algebra checks out inside their model. We reproduced their amplitudes by three independent routes, their key coefficient 3/(25π) exactly, their stochastic-background rate 8πG²ρ²/15, and their ħ bookkeeping.
  • But their model is not a wave travelling away from its source, for two separate reasons:
    1. The amplitudes are complex numbers whose phases they drop. With the phases kept, the rate is 0.25–0.60 of theirs for the polarisation patterns we tried (0.34–0.60 for realistic quadrupole patterns), and exactly zero for a circularly polarised wave.
    2. The quantum state they use carries an incoming spherical wave that collapses onto the source and then re-expands. Most of their probability is generated at that collapse point: about 85% within one wavelength for a spherical shell, and about half within one wavelength (82% within two) for a quadrupole shell.
  • A real outgoing wave makes photon pairs only near its source. The number made beyond a radius r falls as 1/r³. So a wave front does not decay in flight: no “reaction time,” and no depletion along its path. (The tiny depletion of a uniform background, eqs. 14–15, is a different calculation, and it checks out.)
  • Not settled: production right at the source. Inside about λ/2π (318 km for a black-hole merger, about the binary’s own size) nothing was computed. Counting from λ/2π outward, a crude quadrupole estimate already gives 0.3–0.7 of their merger number. So we do not claim their merger numbers are orders of magnitude too large. We claim they describe production at the source while it radiates, not a gravitational wave decaying in vacuum.

1. What Blas and Oller claim

Blas and Oller (“B&O” below) model a gravitational-wave front as a coherent state of N gravitons of frequency ω and bandwidth σ. Their abstract opens: “We show that gravitational waves (GW), treated as coherent graviton states, decay into photon pairs in vacuum. The process, even if suppressed by G², is lifted by two effects combined: the expected factor of the graviton number squared, N², and the coherence of the wave.”

The ingredients are the tree-level amplitudes for two gravitons going to two photons (their eq. 6), with magnitudes 8πG t²/s and 8πG u²/s in terms of the Mandelstam variables, summed coherently over the wave’s graviton modes (eq. 7). The result is a pair-production rate (eq. 8), in natural units (ħ = c = 1):

dP/dt = 3 G² N² ω³ σ² / (25π) B&O eq. 8

From it they derive a “characteristic reaction time” τ (by their eq. 9, the time to the first photon) and a depletion time for the wave (eqs. 9–12); a photon luminosity for compact binaries (their Fig. 2); a rate per volume for a stochastic background, 8πG²ρGW²/15 (eq. 14); an enhancement by CMB photons already present, a factor 1 + 2f with f the CMB occupation number; a cosmological integration over the history of all mergers (eq. 19 and supplement eq. S12); and the analogous decay into ultralight dark-matter scalars (eqs. 16–18).

2. How we checked it

Four analyses, each with its own scripts:

  1. Amplitudes and helicity phases. We rebuilt the full complex amplitude three ways that share no code: a new amplitude module, a spinor-helicity “double copy” (KLT) closed form, and a covariant route with no spinors. The first two agree on the full complex amplitude to 10⁻¹² for random kinematics. The third, whose overall constant is fixed at one point, then reproduces the magnitudes to 10⁻¹⁴ head-on and 9×10⁻⁹ over 200 random configurations, and the convention-free phases exactly.
  2. The space-time structure of the state. What field the coherent state actually carries, and where in space and time the pair probability is generated.
  3. The 2πδ(0) → T step, which turns a probability into a rate.
  4. The stochastic background, CMB stimulation and ultralight dark matter.

Adversarial referees then tried to break each analysis, and a critic re-ran the scripts and checked the report against the logs. We quoted B&O only from their own text, extracted from the PDF.

3. What holds up

Inside B&O’s idealised model, the following are right:

  • the magnitudes of the eq. 6 amplitudes, and the pattern of which ones vanish, provided the labels are read as spin projections on a common axis rather than physical helicities (which matters for polarised waves);
  • the eq. 8 coefficient 3/(25π), exactly, from their own integral;
  • the stochastic-background rate (eq. 14), 8πG²ρ²/15 per unit volume, for any isotropic unpolarised spectrum, and their conclusion that it is utterly negligible (10⁻¹¹⁰ to 5×10⁻¹⁰⁹ photons per cubic metre per second, depending on the background’s size);
  • the drop-out of coherent intermediate gravitons, and the scalar amplitudes and coefficients (7/(450π), 1/16) for the dark-matter channel;
  • the ħ counting: the pair number contains no ħ at fixed classical wave energy and frequency. It is a quartic functional of the wave’s classical strain, so the celebrated N² is ordinary classical phase coherence, not a quantum bonus. Its large size comes from the small focal region discussed in Section 5, not from an extra power of N (the incoherent background rate, eq. 14, is also quadratic in graviton number).

The problems are not arithmetic. They are about whether the quantity computed is the one that describes a real gravitational wave.

4. Problem 1: the helicity phases are dropped

Scattering amplitudes are complex numbers, and for g g → γγ their phases depend on the directions of the outgoing photons. B&O’s eq. 6 lists magnitudes whose labels are spin projections on a common axis rather than physical helicities. That is fine for the magnitudes, but B&O’s eq. 7 then adds the amplitudes as real positive numbers. Combinations of them that don’t depend on phase conventions have phases of +63.9°, −17.1° and −125.8° (one photon direction, three polarisation tilts) rather than 0°, so no choice of convention makes them all real at once.

Keeping the phases matters. Two angular-momentum channels (Jz = +4 and −4) cannot interfere once you integrate over the photons’ azimuth, because their cross term goes as cos 8φ and averages to zero. B&O’s rule keeps that cross term as a constant. As a result:

  • for B&O’s own head-on pair (aligned “plus” polarisations) the rate is 6.4 (in the check’s units), not B&O’s 7.47;
  • the rate depends wrongly on the relative orientation of two linear polarisations (5.33 instead of the physical 6.4 for plus × cross);
  • read literally, eq. 7 gives a nonzero rate for two right-circular gravitons, where the physical rate is exactly zero.

B&O’s “unpolarised isotropic” wave has no unique physical realisation (a smooth polarisation pattern cannot be combed flat over a sphere, the hairy-ball theorem). So we evaluated several real patterns:

Eq. 8 therefore overestimates by 1.7× to 4×, depending on the pattern. A separate inconsistency: eq. 5 gives the wave a Gaussian profile, but eqs. 7 and S6 are evaluated with a top hat. At equal N and equal σ parameter the Gaussian gives a 4π times larger peak rate and a 3.76 times larger total (about 1.5 times at equal rms bandwidth), so the 3/(25π) belongs to the top hat specifically.

5. Problem 2: the wave in the calculation comes in before it goes out

B&O compute an S-matrix probability with the initial state |f⟩ = Ssource|0⟩: the coherent state that the source would create, used as the incoming state of free fields. A free field that agrees with the source’s outgoing radiation at late times must, at early times, have been an incoming spherical wave. That is the Pauli–Jordan (retarded minus advanced) solution. In the calculation the wave converges onto the source’s position, focuses there at t = 0, and re-expands. Numerically, the field is 100% incoming at early times (tω = −30) and 50% incoming at the focus. A real source’s retarded field is never incoming.

That focus is where B&O’s probability comes from:

Why the focus matters: nearly parallel gravitons barely fuse. Two massless particles moving in exactly the same direction have zero invariant mass, s = 0. There is no threshold for g g → γγ, since the photons are massless too, but the amplitude itself shrinks with s: with s + t + u = 0, eq. 6 gives |M| = 8πG t²/s ≤ 8πG s, so the cross-section goes as G²s. Far from its source a wave is locally almost a plane wave, its gravitons nearly parallel, and s is only of order 4ω/r (natural units). Production is strongly suppressed but not zero, which is why the yield falls as a power of r (Section 7) instead of switching off. At the focus, gravitons arrive from every direction, s is large, and fusion is easy. For an exact plane wave, s = 0 and nothing is produced.

To be fair to B&O: they note that far from the source the field tends to the outgoing packet, which is true at late times. They also use the Feynman propagator correctly for the internal graviton. The problem is the external state: the S-matrix integrates over all times, including the fictitious incoming half of the wave.

6. A consequence: eq. 8 is a peak rate, not a sustained one

B&O turn the energy-conserving delta function into a time by the textbook replacement 2πδ(0) → T. Kept exactly instead, it gives a finite total for the whole packet,

Ptotal = (4/25) G² N² ω³ σ pairs,

produced with a time profile R(t) = R(0) sinc⁴(σt/2), where R(0) is exactly B&O’s eq. 8. So eq. 8 is the peak rate, sustained for an effective time 4π/(3σ).

As an average over the packet, B&O’s idealisation is legitimate. What breaks is using it as a constant rate over times much longer than 1/σ. Eq. 9’s photon count exceeds the total photon number, 2Ptotal, for t > 4π/(3σ), and in B&O’s own model the fraction of a packet that is ever converted is (4π/3)/(στh). For their own example (E = M⊙c², ε = 0.15, ω = 10⁴ eV) that fraction is 2.4×10⁻³⁶. That is not depletion.

7. What a real outgoing wave does

To model a physical wave, we kept only the outgoing half of B&O’s packet, switched on beyond a radius rmin, and estimated the pairs it produces with the in-in (expectation-value) formalism, using a scalar stand-in for the gravitational wave rescaled by a gravitational coherence factor of 0.41, which only one module computes:

The rate while the shell passes radius r falls as r⁻⁴, and a quadrupole wave with its near-field terms behaves the same way. This is a formation-length effect, and it undoes the claims that rely on a sustained decay:

  • No reaction time and no depletion of a wave front along its path (eqs. 9–12; their footnote 4, which pictures photons produced on a “sphere of radius τ”).
  • The cosmological integration (eq. 19/S12) overcounts. It lets each merger radiate photons for the whole propagation time. In B&O’s own model the energy per event is the rate times 4π/(3σ), so S12 overcounts by 3σ/(4πH): an order-of-magnitude estimate of about 10²¹ at ω = 10⁻¹⁰ eV today (1.7×10²¹ for a fractional bandwidth ε = 0.1; the factor scales with ε).
  • The “lower bound” from binning is not guaranteed. B&O split a chirping signal into frequency bins and apply eq. 8 to each, calling the result a lower bound. For a bin lasting many wave cycles the exact chirped result is smaller, by about (σtε)²/(4π²) where tε is the time the chirp spends in the bin, so eq. 8 per bin overstates it, by up to about 7,000 inside B&O’s own allowed region. The typical allowed bin is short (σtε ≈ 2.7), where that formula doesn’t apply and the comparison was not computed. So “lower bound” is not guaranteed, and how far off the binning is in practice is contested.

9. What is not settled

  1. Production right at the source (within about λ/2π) is not computed. It needs the binary’s own near-zone field and the coupling of matter to gravitons. This is why we call the comparison with B&O’s binary luminosity undetermined.
  2. The retarded-field argument is analytic only. That the physical field is the retarded, outgoing one when the source is kept inside the calculation was argued, not checked numerically.
  3. Counting pairs “beyond rmin” depends on how the yield is attributed. Smooth switch-on shapes differ by up to 2.2×, and a window-free attribution is about 6 times higher at one wavelength, with the gap growing slowly (as log²). The r⁻³ fall-off holds up to those log factors.
  4. There is no unique physical polarisation pattern for B&O’s isotropic idealisation. The patterns we tried give 0.25–0.60, and a circularly polarised wave gives 0.
  5. The finite-mass scalar channel is incomplete: its physical interference term was not computed.
  6. The coherence factor of 0.41 used in the outgoing-wave numbers (Figure 4 and the merger table) comes from one module with no independent cross-check. No verdict rests on it.
  7. Eq. 6 and the scalar amplitudes (S10) use different label conventions (common-axis spin projections and physical helicities). That is harmless for B&O’s unpolarised states but matters for any polarised application.

Where this could go next

You’ll know better than we do. Some options:

  • A sanity check on Problem 2 by someone who works with QFT in curved or radiating backgrounds. It is the crux for the “decay in flight” conclusion. If the in-state argument is wrong, the focus and r⁻³ findings lose their force, though the phase, profile and finite-total findings stand on their own.
  • Writing to the authors with the specific points (Sections 4–7), or a short “Comment on” paper. A comment on arXiv needs an endorser in the field.
  • Finishing the open piece: production inside the source region, with a real binary model.
  • In the classroom, the argument in Section 5 may be the most usable piece. The g g → γγ cross-section grows with the pair’s invariant mass (σ ∝ G²s), and nearly parallel gravitons have tiny s, so a wave far from its source makes almost no photon pairs (the yield beyond r falls as r⁻³).

Everything is in checks/2026-10-11-blas-oller-gw-decay/: report.md (the full report, quoting B&O verbatim), calc/ (about 35 Python scripts) and notes/ (their output logs). The scripts need numpy, scipy, sympy and mpmath. Fourteen of them import the repository’s qft_second_order.py, and every module output a verdict rests on is cross-checked by an independent route (the coherence factor of 0.41, used only in the outgoing-wave numbers, is not). Run them from the check folder, for example python3 calc/taskC1_exact_Ptot.py. A few take several minutes. The figures here are drawn by summary/make_figures.py from the logged numbers.

Glossary

Coherent state
The quantum state that best resembles a classical wave: a superposition of graviton numbers with a definite average field. A classical gravitational wave is a coherent state with an enormous N (about 3.6×10⁷⁸ for the merger example).
Helicity
A massless particle’s spin along its direction of motion: ±2 for gravitons, ±1 for photons. Circular polarisation means one helicity.
In-out versus in-in
An S-matrix (“in-out”) calculation asks for the probability to go from a state in the far past to one in the far future. An “in-in” calculation asks for the expectation value of an observable, here the number of photon pairs, given the past state. At the order used here the two give the same number for the same initial state. The disagreement with B&O is about which state and field to use (an in-state carrying the Pauli–Jordan field, versus the retarded, outgoing field of a real source), not about in-in versus in-out.
Pauli–Jordan function
The retarded minus the advanced solution. It is a source-free solution of the wave equation, so it can describe a free wave, and it contains an incoming half as well as an outgoing one.
Formation length
The distance a process needs to “complete.” For nearly parallel gravitons the invariant mass is small, the formation length is long, and pair production far from the source is suppressed.
Natural units
ħ = c = 1, so G has units of inverse energy squared and every quantity can be expressed in powers of energy.
