# Verdict on C4 (refuter 2, angle: scale)
claim: A compact positive-energy shell, pushed externally, drags its interior inertial frames so the payload accelerates without felt g-forces: the one merit where it might beat a rocket.
status: final
verdict: weakened
basis: calculation
narrowed: A compact positive-energy shell pushed externally only partly drags its interior frames (κ < 1 wherever the dominant energy condition holds), so the payload still feels a fraction (1 − κ) of the push: roughly half to three quarters of it at the 2024 shell's compactness (proxy estimate). Free fall beside any pushed mass matches or beats this g-shielding, and the shell route costs about 10^22–10^24 times a payload-only rocket's energy.

## Strongest attack

The core claim has three load-bearing parts: "drags its interior inertial frames", "without felt g-forces" and "the one merit where it might beat a rocket". The scale attack measures what the drag must deliver against what a positive-energy shell can deliver. It then asks whether this merit, even granted, beats the alternatives at any achievable cost.

1. **"Without felt g-forces" needs κ → 1, and positive-energy shells don't reach it.** In the only strong-field model of linear dragging (Pfister, Frauendiener & Hengge 2005), the dragging factor is exactly 1 only in the collapse limit R = 2M. A later paper's reading of that model's energy conditions is: "Perfect inertial dragging is only realized in the regions where the dominant energy condition is violated", and "in the part of parameter space obeying the dominant energy condition, perfect inertial dragging is not realized". For a static thin shell, the DEC (p ≤ σ) fails beyond C = 2M/R = 24/25 = 0.96 [calc: runs/2026-10-01-warp-drive-without-negative-energy/calc/falsifier-C4-2_gshield_scale.py §A]. So a shell that obeys the energy conditions the question demands leaves a residual felt acceleration (1 − κ)A > 0. The payload is not free of g-forces; the g-forces are reduced.
2. **How much reduction is achievable.** No strong-field linear-dragging formula is available to quote: the Pfister paper is paywalled, and its eqs. (48)–(49) are not accessible. I therefore use the Brill–Cohen rotational shell as a proxy, since Pfister et al. say their linear results "compare favourably" with it. The proxy reproduces Thirring's 2C/3 in the weak field and gives 1 at C → 1. Its values [calc §B]:

   | Compactness C | κ (proxy) | Felt fraction 1 − κ | Gain 1/(1 − κ) |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | 0.335 (2024 shell, outer radius) | 0.24 | 0.76 | 1.3 |
   | 0.67 (2024 shell, inner radius) | 0.54 | 0.46 | 2.2 |
   | 8/9 (Buchdahl) | 0.80 | 0.20 | 5 |
   | 0.96 (thin-shell DEC edge) | 0.91 | 0.086 | 12 |

   At the published compactness, the crew still feels roughly half to three quarters of the shell's acceleration.
3. **What the gain buys, and its price.** Take a crew limited to 1 g felt and a trip to α Cen (4.37 ly), using an ideal photon rocket with a midpoint flip. All energies are SI [calc §C].
   - A 1 g rocket gives 3.58 yr of crew time.
   - Pushing the shell at 10 g, so the crew feels 1 g, needs f = 0.1, which is only reached at C ≈ 0.96, the DEC edge. It cuts crew time to 0.75 yr, a saving of 2.84 yr.
   - That push needs an exhaust energy of 9.0e47 J, which is 5.0 solar rest masses, against 2.0e25 J for the payload alone.
   - Even the 1 g shell trip costs 1.6e46 J (0.089 M_sun).
   - Photon-push power for the shell at 10 g is 1.3e38 W, which is 3.5e11 L_sun.
   - Earth-frame time cannot drop below 4.37 yr in any case.

   The merit is worth at most a few years of crew time on a stellar trip. It costs about 4.5e22 times (energy ratio of shell to payload) what a rocket carrying the payload alone would spend for the same profile.
4. **The merit is not specific to the shell interior, and the interior is not the best use of the mass.** A payload hanging in free fall beside the same pushed mass M = 4.511e27 kg at distance d = √(GM/A) accelerates with it, feeling no acceleration except small station-keeping thrust. The Newtonian estimates in [calc §D] give:

   | Tow acceleration A | Distance d | Tidal stretch over 2 m |
   |---|---|---|
   | 1 g | 1.75e8 m | 2.3e-8 g |
   | 100 g | 1.75e7 m | 2.3e-5 g |
   | 1000 g | 5.5e6 m | 7.2e-4 g |

   This is effectively κ = 1 with negligible tides, at every A, with no need for compactness, DEC-edge matter or interior dragging. An ordinary-density body (2.26e4 kg/m³, osmium) of 3.5e26 kg, which is 0.078 times the shell mass, tows a payload at 10 g with 2.5e-5 m/s² of tide. So "the one merit where it might beat a rocket" is beaten by the same mass used outside the shell, or by a far less exotic one. The candidate admits that the merit "belongs to any massive shell". The calculation shows it does not even need a shell.

## In principle vs. in practice

- **In principle.**
  - Partial dragging inside a pushed massive shell is real GR (Pfister 2005), so the "drags its interior frames" part stands.
  - "Without felt g-forces" holds only in the collapse limit, which positive-energy (DEC-obeying) matter does not reach in the Pfister model.
  - One exception exists, and its own authors discount it. Arnfinnsson & Grøn build an accelerated C-metric shell with perfect dragging by construction for every m/R. Of that property they write "This is not realistic", and its DEC fails near collapse. It does not rescue the claim.
  - What survives in principle is reduced, not absent, felt acceleration.
- **In practice.**
  - The gain at the published compactness is about 1.3–2.2× (proxy).
  - A tenfold gain needs C at the DEC edge (0.96), above Buchdahl's 8/9 for fluid stars, so it needs a thin elastic shell (D-74 leaves such shells' strong-field stability open).
  - Exploiting a tenfold gain on an α Cen trip costs about 5 M_sun of exhaust energy to save about 2.8 yr of crew time.
  - Free fall beside a pushed mass does the same job better, with no compactness requirement.
  - On scale, the merit does not beat a rocket in any practical sense.

## Corrections to details

- The argument's κ = 0.67 (R = 20 m) and 1.33 (R = 10 m) are linear coefficients. On the strong-field proxy, the corresponding dragging fractions are about 0.24 and 0.54 [calc §B]. So the strong field makes dragging weaker than the linear numbers suggest, not "plausibly strong". The proxy is rotational, not linear; its exact values are uncertain, but its qualitative behaviour (κ < 1 below R = 2M) matches Pfister's linear result as quoted.
- The predicted test "κ → 1 at C ≈ 0.3–0.9" contradicts the cited Pfister result: κ = 1 only at R = 2M, and not in the DEC-obeying region.
- The "one merit" is not unique to shells: free-fall towing beside any pushed mass gives the same effect, with κ effectively 1 [calc §D]. Two caveats apply:
  - the tow position is unstable and needs active station-keeping at small thrust;
  - the mass's exhaust must be canted so it misses the trailing payload.

## What survives and remaining doubt

- **What survives.** A pushed compact shell partly drags its interior frames and reduces the felt acceleration of an enclosed payload, with a flat (tide-free) interior. That is a genuine GR effect, and it beats a rocket only in the narrow sense that a rocket's payload feels the full A.
- **Doubts.**
  - The strong-field κ values here are from a rotational proxy. Pfister's linear formula (eqs. 48–49) was not accessible. PAPER TO REQUEST: doi:10.1088/0264-9381/22/22/007 | A model for linear dragging | the exact strong-field linear dragging factor versus R/M, and where the DEC-obeying region ends.
  - The DEC statement concerns Pfister's charged thin shell pushed by a weak dipolar charge distribution, at first order in acceleration. A thick elastic shell pushed differently could shift κ, since Lynden-Bell, Bičák & Katz stress the pusher's role.
  - None of this changes the scale conclusion. Even κ = 1 would leave the 4.5e22× energy bill and the free-fall tow alternative intact.
