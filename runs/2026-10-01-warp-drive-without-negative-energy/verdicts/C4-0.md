# Verdict on C4 (refuter 0, angle: physics)
claim: A compact positive-energy shell, pushed externally, drags its interior inertial frames so the payload accelerates without felt g-forces: the one merit where it might beat a rocket.
status: final
verdict: weakened
basis: cited-evidence
narrowed: An externally pushed, compact shell of physical matter (DEC obeyed, no horizon) only partially drags its interior frames, so it reduces the payload's felt acceleration to (1 − κ)A with κ < 1 but does not remove it; that comfort gain is not specific to warp geometry, and a rocket-towed compact mass ahead of the payload gives it with 8–10 orders of magnitude less mass.

## Strongest attack
The core claim says the payload "accelerates without felt g-forces", which means perfect translational dragging, κ = 1. Physical models of the strong-field regime put κ = 1 only at the collapse limit, R → 2GM/c², where a horizon forms. They also put it only where the dominant energy condition fails, which is outside "positive energy" as the brief defines it (DEC failure is an "unphysical source", brief l.32). Any compactness the shell can actually reach therefore leaves a residual felt acceleration (1 − κ)A. The payload must be held by a contact force of that size, which is what C4's own "if false" branch predicts, though smaller than an unshielded rocket's.

Three independent lines support this.
1. **Pfister, Frauendiener & Hengge 2005.** This is the strong-field model of a linearly accelerated shell that C4 itself cites. As summarized by Arnfinnsson & Grøn 2014 (quote below), its dragging factor is exactly 1 only at R = 2M with zero charge. In the DEC-obeying part of its parameter space, perfect dragging is not realized.
2. **Lynden-Bell, Bičák & Katz 1999.** This is C4's other cited source. In weak field it gives a free interior body that accelerates "in sympathy but less rapidly than the shell itself". In their strong-field exact solutions, the interior field is reduced only by the g₀₀ ratio, which tends to zero only at infinite redshift.
3. **Brill–Cohen rotational analogue.** Pfister et al. say the translational results "compare favourably" with it. My calculation gives a dragging fraction of 0.24 at the rebuild's C = 0.335 (R₂), 0.80 at Buchdahl, and 0.91 at C = 0.96, where a static thin shell meets its DEC (Zel'dovich) limit. It reaches 1 only at the horizon. For the run's own shell, the payload still feels about 76 % of the push in this analogue.

A second attack hits the clause "the one merit where it might beat a rocket". Removing felt g by gravitational free fall needs no enclosing shell and no warp geometry. Push a compact mass M ahead of the payload at A = GM/d²: the payload free-falls behind it and feels only the tide, 2AL/d. To keep the felt acceleration at 1 % of A for a 10 m payload needs M ≈ 6e17–6e19 kg, at d = 2 km. A shell giving κ ≥ 0.99 needs C ≈ 0.996 (analogue), which is beyond the static-shell DEC limit of 0.96, and M ≈ 6.7e27 kg. The shell is 1e8 to 1e10 times heavier for the same comfort. So the merit, where real, belongs to a rocket-towed mass, which is a rocket system. The shell is the dominated way to get it.

## In principle vs. in practice
- **In principle:**
  - Partial translational dragging by a pushed compact shell is established in GR models, so the interior frames are dragged.
  - Zero felt g-force (κ = 1) with physical matter and no horizon has no model in its favour. One exception is Arnfinnsson & Grøn's C-metric shell, where κ = 1 holds "by construction" for any m/R, which the authors themselves call "not realistic" (p. 16); see Remaining doubt.
  - So "without felt g-forces" fails in principle for DEC-obeying sources, as far as the literature goes, and "with reduced g-forces" survives.
- **In practice:**
  - A large reduction needs C close to the DEC limit of 0.96 (residual about 9 % in the analogue) at masses of order 1e27 kg for 10 m cavities.
  - The full momentum bill, about 4.5e22 times the payload's, is still paid.
  - A rocket-towed compact mass gives the same comfort with 1e8–1e10 times less mass.
  - No practical advantage over a rocket system remains.

## Corrections to details
- The weak-field coefficient is 11GM/(3c²R), not 4GM/(c²R), once retardation is included in de Donder gauge (Lynden-Bell et al. 1999, p. 7). This gives κ_lin = 0.61 at R = 20 m and 1.22 at R = 10 m, not 0.67 and 1.33. The lens's own math check flagged the 4M/R version as ignoring retardation (M-MECHANIST-10).
- Lynden-Bell et al. 1999 is cited for an "opposing interior field" in the EM analogue. That is correct for EM, but for gravity the same paper's conclusion is "less rapidly than the shell itself". It supports partial dragging, not g-free acceleration.
- The prediction "κ → 1 at C ≈ 0.3–0.9" is not supported. The only strong-field translational and rotational results put the dragging fraction well below 1 there: about 0.24–0.80 in the rotational analogue.

## What survives and remaining doubt
- **What survives:** an externally pushed compact shell does drag its interior frames, and the payload feels less than the push, (1 − κ)A, with κ growing towards 1 only as the shell nears its horizon.
- **Model dependence:** κ depends on how the shell is pushed and on its stresses. Arnfinnsson & Grøn's exact C-metric source has κ = 1 at every m/R, with the WEC obeyed and the DEC failing only near the collapse limit. It is driven by the C-metric's conical strut, its pressure is angle-dependent, and the authors call the m/R-independence unrealistic. No time-dependent, DEC-obeying start from rest has been shown to reach κ = 1. This is why the verdict is weakened rather than refuted.
- **Analogue only:** my κ(C) numbers come from the rotational Brill–Cohen analogue, not the translational strong-field solution. Pfister's figure 4 (paywalled) would settle the translational κ(C) under the DEC.
