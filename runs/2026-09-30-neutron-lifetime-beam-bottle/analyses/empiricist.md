# Analysis: empiricist (Hume)
status: final

## Method applied
Separate what was observed (instrument, conditions, uncertainty, replication) from what was inferred; set base rates from how past disagreements between established precision methods ended; look for analogues; state what an honest skeptic would demand and whether it exists.

## Findings

F1. Base rate for large disagreements among careful measurements. Bailey 2017 analysed 41 000 measurements of 3200 quantities: [new: Bailey 2017, Royal Society Open Science 4, 160600, doi:10.1098/rsos.160600 (published version via Europe PMC), full-text, "Outliers are common, with 5 σ disagreements up to five orders of magnitude more frequent than naively expected. Uncertainty-normalized differences between multiple measurements of the same quantity are consistent with heavy-tailed Student's t -distributions that are often almost Cauchy"] and [same source, full-text, "Published scientific measurements typically have non-Gaussian almost-Cauchy ν ∼ 2−4 Student's t -error distributions, with up to 10% of results in disagreement by greater than 5 σ . These heavy tails occur in even the most careful modern research"]. Bailey's examples of how such failures ended are all unrecognised systematics or mistakes: [same source, full-text, "In 1996, a team from PTB ... reported a measurement of G N that differed by 50 σ from the accepted value; it took 8 years to track down the cause—a plausible but erroneous assumption about their electrostatic torque transmitter unit"]; [same, "A 6.5 σ difference between the CODATA2006 and CODATA2010 fine-structure constant values was due to a mistake in the calculation of some eighth-order terms"]. Observation: a 4–5σ gap between two methods is, by this base rate, an ordinary event for frontier precision measurements, and it says nothing by itself about new physics.

F2. The closest analogue, the orthopositronium (o-Ps) decay-rate puzzle, is a disappearance-rate measurement where an early discrepancy was traced to systematics in one method: [new: Gidley et al. 1978, Phys. Rev. Lett. 40, 737, abstract, "The vacuum decay rate of orthopositronium formed in a gas is determined, after extrapolation to zero gas density, to be 7.056±0.007 μsec−1. The discovery of two systematic effects in a previous powder experiment is discussed. A new measurement of the decay rate in SiO2 powders yields results in agreement with the new gas value."] (the later 1990s round of the same puzzle is F3).

F3. The o-Ps puzzle's second round is the sharper analogue. Two methods with different systematics agreed with each other and disagreed with QED: [new: Westbrook et al. 1989, Phys. Rev. A 40, 5489, abstract, "The vacuum decay rate λT of orthopositronium (13S1) formed in a gas has been measured to be λT=7.0514±0.0014μs−1"]; [new: Nico et al. 1990, Phys. Rev. Lett. 65, 1344, abstract, "The o-Ps decay rate λT is determined to be 7.0482±0.0016 μs−1 after extrapolation to infinite cavity size. ... Subject to radically different systematic effects, it corrob…"]. The resolution: [new: Vallery et al. 2003, Phys. Rev. Lett. 90, 203402, abstract (INSPIRE API, full abstract), "A focused beam of positrons incident on a special nanoporous silica film produces near-thermal energy o−Ps in vacuum that is slow enough to be virtually free of perturbing interactions. The fitted decay rate requires only a 500 ppm correction for nonthermal o−Ps effects. The new value of λT=7.0404(10)(8) μs−1 is in excellent agreement with theory."]. A Tokyo method built to remove the thermalization ambiguity [new: Asai et al. 1995, Phys. Lett. B 357, 475, abstract, "The decay rate can be determined without the ambiguities of the thermalization nor of the extrapolation, which might have been sources of the main systematic errors in all the previous experiments."] had pointed there first. Lessons for this case: (a) agreement between two methods with "radically different systematic effects" did not protect both from sharing one unmodelled effect (the thermalization of the stored population), so the agreement of material and magnetic bottles is weaker evidence than it looks; (b) the discrepancy lasted about 13 years (1990 to 2003) and was ended by a new technique that removed the suspect effect by design, not by re-analysing old data; (c) the o-Ps rate is also a disappearance rate from a stored population, like a bottle lifetime. (Author overlap noted without inference: J.S. Nico of the 1990 o-Ps paper is also on NIST BL1 [D-20].)

F4. Two recent precision puzzles in the same reference class ended without new physics. Proton radius: [new: WebSearch summary of Gasparian 2024 overview, physicsworld and arXiv:2501.11195, search-summary, SUMMARY "Recent results from improved electronic hydrogen spectroscopy measurements obtain values of the radius in agreement with that obtained from muonic atoms ... The radius of the proton is the small value of about 0.84 fm", "confirmed in ordinary hydrogen via spectroscopy as well as a scattering experiment" at JLab]. That puzzle (5.6σ in 2010) ended with the newer, cleaner method (muonic) right and the older ordinary-hydrogen and scattering ensemble wrong. Muon g−2: [new: WebSearch summary of Muon g-2 Theory Initiative White Paper 2025 and CERN Courier, search-summary, SUMMARY "Adopting the lattice-informed HVP inputs results in a major upward shift of the total SM prediction to 116592033(62) × 10⁻¹¹ ... the difference is 38(63) × 10⁻¹¹ ... no statistically significant tension"]. That ~5σ anomaly ended when one theory input (the data-driven e⁺e⁻ HVP) proved unreliable, a systematic in one input; the measurement itself replicated (FNAL agreed with BNL).

F5. Which partition carries the disagreement (observations only: 10 current results, stat and sys in quadrature, J-PARC symmetrised to 4.16 s; Sussex–ILL is an unverified lead [D-22]) [calc: runs/2026-09-30-neutron-lifetime-beam-bottle/calc/lens-empiricist_partitions.py]:
- beam (proton + electron) vs bottle: between χ² = 16.35/1 (4.04σ), and 28.5/8 is left within the groups (p = 3.8×10⁻⁴);
- proton-counting vs everything else: between χ² = 21.66/1 (4.65σ), with 23.2/8 left within (p = 0.003);
- BL1 alone vs everything else: between χ² = 16.78/1 (4.10σ);
- four classes (proton beam 887.97 ± 2.04 s; J-PARC 877.2 ± 4.16 s; material 880.03 ± 0.49 s (χ² 8.2/4, S = 1.43); magnetic 877.83 ± 0.29 s): between χ² = 36.55/3 (5.43σ), with only 8.33/6 left within (p = 0.22).
Reading: "proton-counting vs the rest" fits better than "beam vs bottle", because J-PARC sits with the bottles. But the data hold a second, independent disagreement: material vs magnetic bottles, between χ² = 14.81/1 (3.85σ), which is Gravitrap vs UCNτ at 3.80σ (+3.68 ± 0.97 s). Two anomalies, not one: a 1.1% one resting on proton counting and a 0.4% one inside the storage class.

F6. How much the main anomaly rests on one experiment [same calc]. Proton-counting vs rest, between χ²: 21.66 with both proton-beam results, 16.89 without Sussex–ILL, 4.96 (2.2σ) without BL1. Without UCNτ the tension is still 3.86σ (rest mean 879.89 s). So the storage side is replicated across several groups and two trap technologies, while the beam side is one apparatus and one data set (the 2005 BL1 data re-analysed in 2013 [D-07]) plus one older result at 2.35σ from UCNτ on its own. Observationally the anomaly is "BL1 against a replicated storage value", and BL1's 2013 fluence re-calibration moved it by +1.4 s [D-20], away from the bottles.

F7. J-PARC's discriminating power as observed [same calc; D-23, D-24]. As quoted, J-PARC sits 0.14σ from UCNτ and 2.24σ from the proton-beam mean (2.15σ from BL1). Its four run conditions give χ² = 19.6/3 on statistical errors alone (p = 2×10⁻⁴; the paper quotes 15.8/3). If that scatter is treated as an unexplained systematic by inflating its statistical error by √(15.8/3) = 2.29, J-PARC is 1.81σ from the proton beam and 0.11σ from UCNτ. The three mutually consistent conditions average 869.6 ± 2.5 s (stat only), while the outlier condition (50 kPa/new SFC) gives 884.8 ± 2.4 s, only 2.9 s below BL1. Observation: J-PARC favours "proton-counting-specific" over "any beam" at about 2σ, not more, and its internal scatter is as large as the effect it is asked to decide.

F8. Size of the gap from this combination: 9.55 s, fraction 1 − τ_bottle/τ_p-beam = 0.0108, missing rate 1.23×10⁻⁵ s⁻¹ [same calc]. UCNτ's per-year values have χ² = 6.67/4 (p = 0.16, S = 1.29) [D-27], so its internal scatter is ordinary.

F9. Heavy-tail base rate applied [same calc; F1]. The probability of one comparison exceeding z = 4.65 is 3.3×10⁻⁶ for a Gaussian but 0.043, 0.019 and 0.0096 for Student-t with ν = 2, 3 and 4 (unit scale), which is Gaussian-equivalent 2.0–2.6σ. Caveat: Bailey fits a scale as well as ν, and the neutron-lifetime community's error estimates may be better than his average. The point stands, though: under the empirical error distribution of precision physics, a 4.6σ two-method gap is roughly a 1-in-50 to 1-in-100 event per comparison, not 1 in 300 000. Across the many precision comparisons being made in physics, cases like it are expected. This makes "a systematic in one method" the default reading and demands far more than 4.6σ before new physics is preferred.

F10. The neutron lifetime's own history is a shift of about the same size as today's gap, inside the storage method: [new: Serebrov & Fomin 2010, arXiv:1005.4312, full-text, "A new most accurate result of the measurem ent of neutron lifetime [Phys. Lett. B 605 (2005) 72] 878.5 ± 0.8 s differs from the world average value [Phys. Lett. B 667 (2008) 1] 885.7 ± 0.8 s by 6.5 standard deviations. ... Systematic errors of about 6 s are found in each of the experiments."] The earlier bottle results (Mampe 1989; Arzumanov 2000) were too long by about 6 s because of unmodelled wall-loss systematics. Observations that follow: (a) storage experiments have a documented record of ~6 s unrecognised systematics, but in the too-long direction (loss corrections that were too small in the extrapolation), which is the opposite sign to the unidentified extra loss a "bottle systematic" candidate now needs; (b) before 2005 the proton-beam value agreed with a world average later shown to be wrong. The beam has never agreed with a post-2005 storage result, so "beam replicated" was always partly agreement with bottle values later revised. [new: WebSearch summary, search-summary, SUMMARY "As a sole and severely discrepant value, it was not included in the PDG average until 2012 when additional low values were reported, and the 2012 average was τ_n = 880.1 ± 1.1 where the uncertainty has been scaled by a factor of 1.8."] The community took about 7 years to accept the lower value, and it did so because independent storage experiments replicated it (MAMBO II, Steyerl, Arzumanov 2015, then magnetic traps [D-29, D-30, D-27]). That is the pattern replication produces, and the beam side has not yet had it.

F11. Reactor antineutrino anomaly (a shared-input case) [new: WebSearch summary of Kopeikin et al. PRD 104, L071301 (2021) and arXiv:2110.06820, search-summary, SUMMARY "the ratio of the two fluxes is 1.054±0.002 smaller than that of the ILL measurement on which the HM is based ... adjusted predictions are now consistent with the Daya Bay and STEREO reactor experiments ... reduction to about 1σ"]. A 2026 preprint titled "Revival of the Reactor Antineutrino Anomaly" (arXiv:2605.10353, title only seen) shows such closures can reopen. Relevance: an absolute normalisation input calibrated long ago (there, the ILL β spectra; here, BL1's ⁶Li fluence monitor and Alpha-Gamma calibration [D-20, D-07]) is a classic hiding place for a percent-level error.

F12. No BL2 result is public as of this search [new: WebSearch, search-summary, SUMMARY "Earlier projections indicated experimental running of BL2 should be complete in 2023, with publication following on a few year time scale and a targeted uncertainty of better than 2 s"; also "Detection of molecular hydrogen in a neutron beam lifetime experiment" PRC (arXiv:2506.01682)], which matches [D-100] and [D-17]. The direct replication of the beam side is still pending, about 13 years after Yue 2013.

F13. A second method-dependent split in the same system, recorded as an observation, not a mechanism. The λ values from the electron β asymmetry (PERKEO III, UCNA, PERKEO II; mutually consistent [D-15]) give an SM τ_β of 878.50 ± 0.88 s (PERKEO III), 877.60 ± 2.36 s and 880.34 ± 1.40 s, which sit with the storage lifetimes. The proton-recoil a-coefficient value from aSPECT 2024 gives 889.58 ± 3.20 s, which sits with BL1 [U-01; D-42; D-74]. So the two measurements that rest on detecting decay protons at high precision (BL1's lifetime and aSPECT's proton spectrum) both land "long", and the electron-detecting measurements (J-PARC, PERKEO III) land "short". The counter-example is aCORN (proton–electron coincidence, 874.86 ± 7.07 s [U-01]), whose error is too large to weigh much. This is a pattern among three or four data points and could be chance (λ tension ≈3–3.5σ [D-42]). It does two things. It makes the SM arbitration conditional on which λ method is right [D-74], and it hints that detecting low-energy protons with silicon detectors in strong fields is a shared hiding place worth auditing: an inference, not an observation. Note that under dark decay the aSPECT λ would be the one that is right. That dark-decay-plus-aSPECT world still predicts J-PARC ≈ 888 s, and J-PARC does not show it (F7).

F14. The precision an honest skeptic needs [calc: runs/2026-09-30-neutron-lifetime-beam-bottle/calc/lens-empiricist_skeptic.py]. To place one new measurement at 888 s or at 878.4 s (gap 9.56 s), it needs σ ≤ 3.18 s for 3σ and ≤ 1.90 s for 5σ (storage error 0.25 s included). J-PARC 2024's 4.16 s gives 2.29σ. An electron-counting beam (LiNA [D-102], target ~1 s) or in-bottle β counting (UCNProBe [D-106], 1–2 s, search-summary) at 1 s would separate "proton-counting systematic" from "proton-less dark decay" at 9.3σ, and at 2 s at 4.7σ. A new proton-counting beam (BL2 [D-100]; BL3 target 0.3 s [D-101]) that lands at the storage value with σ = 1 s would disagree with BL1 at 3.8σ; BL1's own 2.25 s error, not the new experiment, limits that test (4.1σ at σ = 0.3 s). A BL2/BL3 value at 888 s with σ ≤ 1 s would put the beam class ≥ 9σ from storage, with the replication the beam side now lacks. The material–magnetic sub-anomaly (2.20 s) needs a new material bottle at ≤ 0.67 s (3σ) or ≤ 0.33 s (5σ). History: PDG 2006 against Serebrov 2005 was 7.2 s (6.4σ in my quadrature, 6.5σ as the authors quote), 0.75 of today's gap.

In the sourced reference class, 0 of 7 cases ended in new physics. Laplace's rule gives a crude posterior of (0 + 1)/(7 + 2) = 1/9 ≈ 0.11 that the next such case does. Adding cases I know only from background knowledge and did not verify in this run (the OPERA superluminal timing, the 17 keV neutrino, BICEP2, the 750 GeV diphoton excess, the CDF W mass, the Cs-vs-Rb α disagreement, which is still open) adds no counter-example. With n ≈ 12 the figure is (0 + 1)/(12 + 2) = 1/14 ≈ 0.07, but I weight those unsourced cases qualitatively only. Two sub-patterns matter here. (i) In the resolved cases, the method that was **newer, more precise and independently replicated** was right (muonic radius, low-temperature bottles, near-thermal o-Ps), and the older, less-replicated method carried the error. (ii) One case (o-Ps) had **two methods wrong together** through a shared property of the stored population, so replication across trap types lowers, but does not remove, the chance that storage is wrong. Resolution took 7 to 20 years and came from a new technique that removed the suspect effect by design.

### 3. Analogues and what they suggest

- **o-Ps (F2, F3):** a stored-population disappearance rate, a proposed invisible exotic decay, then a mundane shared systematic. It suggests testing storage for a population-evolution effect common to both trap types (energy spectrum evolution, marginally trapped or upscattered UCN). The magnetic-trap UCNτ has direct in-situ tests of exactly this (dagger, variable-height cleaning [D-10]), and they found no such effect at the 0.2 s level. So the analogue keeps a bottle systematic alive only weakly.
- **1989–2012 neutron bottle shift (F10):** storage systematics of ~6 s did happen. Their sign was too long, opposite to what is needed now.
- **Reactor flux (F11):** a percent-level error in an old absolute normalisation. The analogue is BL1's absolute ⁶Li fluence calibration [D-20], the only piece of BL1 that the 2013 re-analysis changed, by +1.4 s.
- **Proton detection in λ (F13):** the internal analogue is aSPECT against PERKEO III. Proton-detection experiments land on the "long" side in both observables.

## What would change my mind

- BL2 or BL3 reporting ≥ 886 s with σ ≤ 1.5 s from an apparatus with a new fluence monitor and a characterised residual gas: that moves weight from A to C or B, and the beam side would then be replicated.
- An electron-counting beam (LiNA) or UCNProBe reading ~888 s at ≤ 2 s: that strongly favours C (or B, if UCNProBe agrees).
- Nab or PERC confirming aSPECT's a-coefficient λ (≈ −1.267) and excluding PERKEO III's: that removes the SM arbitration for storage and revives C.
- τSPECT or UCNτ+ reading ≥ 881 s: that makes the storage split the story and strengthens B and E.
- A J-PARC erratum or journal version, or Desai's pressure effect shown to move J-PARC by ≥ 5 s upward: that removes the ~2σ J-PARC leg of A.
